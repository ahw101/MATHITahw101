The 6 times table

Year 4 12 facts 6 × 6 = 36 Practise this →

The 6 times table arrives in Year 4, when the national curriculum expects all tables to 12 × 12 to be secure. It sits in the awkward middle of the set: too big to count up to comfortably, without the obvious hook that 5, 9 and 10 enjoy.

It does have two solid routes in. It is double the 3 times table, and — less well known — every even multiplier produces an answer ending in that same digit.

Patterns in the 6 times table

1

Even multipliers keep their own last digit

6 × 2 = 12, 6 × 4 = 24, 6 × 6 = 36, 6 × 8 = 48. The answer ends in the digit you multiplied by, and the tens digit is half of it.

So 6 × 8: half of 8 is 4, giving 48. Six of the twelve facts fall out of this immediately, and it is the single most useful thing to know about this table.

2

Every answer is double a multiple of 3

3 × 7 = 21, so 6 × 7 = 42. If the threes are secure, the sixes are one doubling away.

3

Multiples of 6 are divisible by both 2 and 3

A number divides by 6 exactly when it is even and its digits add to a multiple of 3. 114 is even, and 1 + 1 + 4 = 6, so 114 divides by 6. This combines the tests from the 2 and 3 tables.

The fastest route

Halve the multiplier, stick it in front

For even multipliers only: halve the number, then write the number itself after it. 6 × 8 → half of 8 is 4 → 48. 6 × 6 → 3 → 36. 6 × 12 → 6 → 72. Correct every time for 2, 4, 6, 8 and 12.

For odd multipliers, go via the nearest even one: 6 × 7 = 6 × 6 + 6 = 36 + 6 = 42. Or double the 3 times table fact.

The facts that stick

Not every fact needs the same amount of practice. These are the ones that slow children down in the 6 times table, and what to do about each.

6 × 7 = 42

Consistently one of the two least-recalled facts in the whole grid. 6 × 6 = 36, add 6 more. Or double 21.

6 × 8 = 48

Use the even trick: half of 8 is 4, so 48. Much more reliable than trying to recall it cold.

6 × 9 = 54

6 × 10 = 60, take away 6. Or use the 9 table: the digits of 54 add to 9.

Division facts

Every multiplication fact is two division facts. A child who knows 6 × 7 but stalls on 42 ÷ 6 has learnt the table in one direction only — which is exactly the gap that shows up later in fractions and simplifying.

36

6 squared

6 × 6 = 36

The square number in this table. It is the area of a square with sides of 6 cm, and the one fact here that reappears in Year 6 work on square and cube numbers and in area questions.

Where the 6 times table turns up

Tables learnt in context are recalled faster than tables learnt as a list. These are the places a child will actually meet multiples of 6.

  • Half a dozen. Eggs come in sixes. Four boxes is 24 eggs — a genuine × 6 problem most children have seen.
  • Hexagons. Six sides each. Honeycomb, bolt heads, floor tiles.
  • Dice. Six faces, so two dice give 12 outcomes per face and 36 combinations in total — 6 squared.
  • Six-packs and multipacks. Supermarket packaging is a steady supply of × 6 and ÷ 6 questions.

Getting it secure

Research on the Year 4 Multiplication Tables Check consistently shows that 6 × 7, 6 × 8, 7 × 8 and 8 × 9 are the slowest facts nationally. The sixes contain two of those four, which makes this table worth disproportionate practice time.

The useful insight is that children do not need to recall every fact at the same speed. Six facts in this table come free from the even-digit trick. Concentrate the effort on 6 × 7 and 6 × 9, and the table as a whole stops being a problem.

Questions people ask

What is the 6 times table?
6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42, 48, 54, 60, 66, 72 — the multiples of 6 up to 6 × 12.
What is the trick for the 6 times table?
For even multipliers, halve the number and put it in front: 6 × 8 gives 4 then 8, so 48. It works for 2, 4, 6, 8 and 12.
How can you tell if a number divides by 6?
It must be even and its digits must add to a multiple of 3. 114 is even and 1 + 1 + 4 = 6, so it divides by 6.
Why is 6 × 7 so hard to remember?
Neither number has an end-digit pattern and the answer, 42, has no obvious link to either. It is reliably one of the slowest facts in national testing — it usually needs deliberate practice.
