Shape & geometry

Coordinates, translation and reflection

Years 4–6 5 min read Practise this →

Coordinates give every point on a grid a unique address: a pair of numbers in brackets, x first and y second. The point (3, 5) means three across and five up from the origin at (0, 0).

The order is not negotiable, and getting it backwards is the single biggest source of lost marks in the topic. The standard mnemonic is along the corridor, then up the stairs.

The method, step by step

  1. 1

    Read x first, then y

    (3, 5) is 3 along the horizontal x-axis and 5 up the vertical y-axis. Alphabetical order helps: x comes before y.

  2. 2

    Start every reading at the origin

    (0, 0) is where the axes cross. All coordinates are measured from there.

  3. 3

    Count grid lines, not squares

    Points sit on the intersections of the lines, not inside the boxes. Counting squares gives an answer one out.

  4. 4

    Handle four quadrants with signs

    Right and up are positive; left and down are negative. So (−4, 2) is four left and two up, and (−3, −5) is three left and five down.

  5. 5

    Translate by adding to the coordinates

    “3 right and 2 down” adds 3 to every x and subtracts 2 from every y. (5, 7) becomes (8, 5). The shape never changes size or orientation.

  6. 6

    Reflect by flipping one sign

    Reflecting in the y-axis negates x: (4, 3) → (−4, 3). Reflecting in the x-axis negates y: (4, 3) → (4, −3).

Worked examples

Where it usually goes wrong

✕ Reading (3, 5) as 3 up and 5 across.

✓ x always comes first. Along the corridor, then up the stairs — and x is before y in the alphabet.

✕ Counting squares rather than lines.

✓ Points lie on the intersections of grid lines. Counting the squares between them gives a value one too small.

✕ Translating left or down by adding.

✓ Left and down are negative directions, so they subtract. Only right and up add.

✕ Reflecting in the x-axis by changing x.

✓ Reflecting in the x-axis flips the shape vertically, so it is the y coordinate that changes sign.

The four quadrants

Extending the axes in both directions gives four regions, numbered anticlockwise from the top right:

  • First — x positive, y positive: (4, 3)
  • Second — x negative, y positive: (−4, 3)
  • Third — both negative: (−4, −3)
  • Fourth — x positive, y negative: (4, −3)

Year 4 works in the first quadrant only. All four arrive in Year 6, which is also where negative numbers need to be secure — the two topics reinforce one another.

Translation keeps everything but position

Translating a shape slides it. Lengths, angles, area and orientation are all unchanged — only the position moves. That is why you can translate by simply adding the same pair of numbers to every vertex.

A useful check: after translating, the shape should look identical and sit the same way up. If it has flipped, you have reflected it by mistake.

Where this is heading

Coordinates are the bridge between number and shape, and they lead directly into graphs at secondary. Plotting y = 2x + 1 is exactly the same skill: generate pairs of numbers, plot them, join them up.

Children who are fluent with the four quadrants in Year 6 find straight-line graphs in Year 7 almost free, which makes this a topic worth over-practising.

Questions people ask

Which number comes first in a coordinate pair?
The x coordinate, which is the horizontal one. Along the corridor, then up the stairs.
What is the origin?
The point (0, 0) where the two axes cross. Every coordinate is measured from it.
What is the difference between translation and reflection?
Translation slides a shape without turning or flipping it. Reflection produces a mirror image across a line.
When are the four quadrants taught?
Year 6 in England. Year 4 introduces coordinates in the first quadrant only.
