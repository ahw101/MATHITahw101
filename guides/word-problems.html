How to solve word problems

Years 2–6 7 min read Practise this →

Children who can calculate perfectly well often lose marks on reasoning papers, and it is almost never the arithmetic. The hard part is deciding what sum to do.

Word problems are a reading skill as much as a maths one. The good news is that a small, repeatable routine handles most of them, and it can be taught directly rather than picked up by luck.

The method, step by step

  1. 1

    Read it twice before writing anything

    First read for the story, second read for the numbers. Starting to calculate during the first read is how children end up answering a question that was never asked.

  2. 2

    Underline the actual question

    Usually the last sentence. “How much change?” and “How much did she spend?” sit in the same problem and have different answers.

  3. 3

    Decide the operation, then write the number sentence

    Write it out before calculating: 4 × 85 = ?. Committing to the sum separates the thinking from the arithmetic so a slip in one does not hide the other.

  4. 4

    Estimate first

    “About 4 × 80, so roughly 320.” An estimate catches a misplaced digit instantly and takes five seconds.

  5. 5

    Answer the question in words, with units

    “340 g”, not “340”. Then reread the question and check your answer actually responds to it.

Worked examples

Where it usually goes wrong

✕ Grabbing the numbers and guessing an operation.

✓ Read the whole problem first. The numbers often appear in a different order from the calculation you need.

✕ Trusting keywords blindly.

✓ ‘Altogether’ usually means add, but “there are 5 boxes with 6 in each, how many altogether?” is multiplication. Keywords are a hint, not a rule.

✕ Stopping after step one of a two-step problem.

✓ Multi-step questions hide the second step in the final sentence. After calculating, reread the question.

✕ Dropping the units.

✓ An unlabelled number can lose a mark even when it is right, and mixing grams with kilograms mid-problem loses it outright.

Use a bar model when you are stuck

Drawing the problem beats staring at it. A bar model shows a bar for the whole and segments for the parts, and it makes the operation obvious:

  • Whole known, one part known → subtract.
  • Both parts known → add.
  • Several equal parts → multiply or divide.
  • Comparison → two bars, one above the other, with the difference marked.

It is the standard approach in the Singapore-influenced schemes most English primaries now use, and it works particularly well for ratio and fraction problems where the arithmetic alone is opaque.

“Each” appears in both multiplication and division rows, which is the whole problem with teaching keywords as a shortcut. Use them to form a hypothesis, then check it against the story.

Multi-step problems

Most Year 6 reasoning marks come from problems with two or three steps. The technique is to write each step as its own number sentence, on its own line, with a short label:

Total cost: 3 × 4.25 = 12.75   Change: 20 − 12.75 = 7.25

This earns method marks even if the arithmetic slips, and it stops children losing track halfway through. It is also how working is expected to be set out at secondary, so the habit pays off twice.

Practising the right thing

If a child can calculate but freezes on word problems, more arithmetic practice will not help. Instead, give them the problem and ask only for the number sentence — no answer required. Ten of those in five minutes trains the skill that is actually missing.

Reversing it works too: hand them a calculation such as 120 ÷ 8 and ask them to invent a story that fits. Children who can do that have genuinely understood what division means.

Questions people ask

How do you solve a maths word problem?
Read it twice, underline the question, write the calculation as a number sentence, estimate, calculate, then check your answer against the question with units.
Are keyword lists reliable?
Only as a hint. ‘Each’ can signal multiplication or division, so always check the keyword against what the story is actually describing.
What is a bar model?
A drawing that shows the whole as a bar and the parts as segments. It makes the right operation visible, and is widely used in English primary schools.
Why does my child get the sums right but the word problems wrong?
The barrier is comprehension, not calculation. Practise writing the number sentence without solving it — that isolates the step they are finding hard.
