Measurement

Telling the time, and calculating with it

Years 1–6 6 min read Practise this →

Time is the one measurement on the primary curriculum that is not metric, and that is exactly why children find it hard. Everywhere else ten of something makes the next thing up. Here it is sixty seconds to a minute, sixty minutes to an hour, twenty-four hours to a day and seven days to a week.

None of those are powers of ten, so the place-value shortcuts that make kilometres easy are no help at all. Time needs its own method, and that method is counting on.

The method, step by step

  1. 1

    Read the hour hand first

    The short hand gives the hour. It moves continuously, so at twenty to four it sits between 3 and 4 — and the hour you say is the one it has passed, not the one it is approaching. This is the single commonest analogue error.

  2. 2

    Then read the minute hand in fives

    Each number on the face is five minutes. Count round in fives from the 12, then add the small marks. The 7 is 35 minutes.

  3. 3

    Past to half, then to after half

    Up to 30 minutes we say “past”; after 30 we usually say “to” the next hour. 3:40 is “twenty to four”, because there are twenty minutes left until 4 o'clock.

  4. 4

    Convert to 24-hour by adding 12 after midday

    Morning times keep their number and gain a leading zero: 9:15 am is 09:15. Afternoon and evening times add 12: 3:40 pm is 15:40. Midnight is 00:00 and midday is 12:00.

  5. 5

    Find durations by counting on, never by subtracting

    Jump to the next whole hour, then in whole hours, then the remaining minutes. Column subtraction fails on times because the columns are not base ten.

Worked examples

Where it usually goes wrong

✕ Using column subtraction: 16:20 − 14:35 treated as 1620 − 1435 = 185 minutes.

✓ Minutes are base 60, not base 100. Count on through the next whole hour instead — the answer is 1 h 45, which is 105 minutes.

✕ Reading 3:40 as ‘twenty to three’.

✓ The hour hand has already passed 3 and is heading for 4, so it is twenty to four.

✕ Adding 12 to a morning time when converting to 24-hour.

✓ Only times from 1 pm onwards gain 12. 9:15 am is simply 09:15.

✕ Writing 2 hours 90 minutes.

✓ Exchange 60 minutes for an hour, exactly as you would exchange ten ones for a ten. It is 3 hours 30 minutes.

The number line is the right tool

Draw a line, mark the start time at the left and the end time at the right, then hop: to the next whole hour, along in hours, then the leftover minutes. Add the hops.

It looks childish and it is the method secondary teachers still recommend, because it never produces the base-60 errors that subtraction does. For 09:48 to 12:23 the hops are 12 minutes, 2 hours, 23 minutes — giving 2 hours 35 minutes.

Timetables

Timetable questions are the most common time question in KS2 reasoning papers, and they are really three skills at once: reading a table accurately, converting between clock formats, and calculating a duration.

Two things catch children out. First, a column may skip a stop — a dash means the train does not stop there, not that it takes no time. Second, journeys that cross midday or midnight: 23:40 to 00:15 is 35 minutes, not an error.

What is expected, and when

Year 1 reads o'clock and half past. Year 2 adds quarter past and quarter to and counts in five-minute intervals. Year 3 reads to the nearest minute, uses Roman numeral clock faces and meets the 24-hour clock. Year 4 converts between analogue and digital including 12- and 24-hour formats. Years 5 and 6 solve multi-step duration and timetable problems.

Analogue reading is worth maintaining even in a digital household — clock faces appear in every SATs paper, and a round clock also teaches fractions of a turn, which feeds straight into angles.

Facts worth knowing by heart

  • 60 seconds = 1 minute, 60 minutes = 1 hour, 24 hours = 1 day
  • 7 days = 1 week, 52 weeks ≈ 1 year, 365 days = 1 year (366 in a leap year)
  • A leap year is any year divisible by 4, except century years not divisible by 400 — so 2000 was a leap year and 1900 was not
  • “Thirty days hath September, April, June and November”

Questions people ask

Why do children find telling the time so hard?
Because a clock face shows two different scales at once — hours and minutes — using hands of different lengths on the same numbers, and because time is base 60 rather than base 10.
How do I work out a duration that crosses midnight?
Count on to midnight, then on again from 00:00. From 23:40 to 00:15 is 20 minutes plus 15 minutes, so 35 minutes.
Is 12:00 am midday or midnight?
Midnight. Midday is 12:00 pm. The ambiguity is real enough that timetables normally use 00:00 and 12:00 in 24-hour format instead.
When should a child read an analogue clock fluently?
To the nearest minute by the end of Year 3 in England, with 12- and 24-hour conversion secure by the end of Year 4.
